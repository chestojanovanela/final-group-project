How do your results compare with what researchers expect?

Pictures: Studies of concrete objects often find a memory advantage for pictures over written words, including in some tasks involving pairs of items. This is called the picture superiority effect. A picture may provide a visual representation as well as a verbal label, creating potential routes to recall. Pictures can also be more distinctive than similar-looking printed words. Researchers disagree about how much each explanation contributes.

Reading: Printed words provide the exact verbal label, which can help you rehearse it or create a meaningful association. Because our memory test uses written cues and typed answers, reading may also benefit from a closer match between studying and testing. Reading does not prevent you from forming mental images.

Listening: Hearing words provides sound-based information that can support verbal rehearsal, especially if you repeat the words or make a meaningful connection. Spoken information is temporary, so attention, the pace of presentation and audio quality can affect recall. Listening is useful for some materials, but there is no established universal order of effectiveness for all three formats.

No validated average or percentile exists for this exact StudyLab task, and we do not yet have an approved group benchmark to compare your score with. Therefore, your score cannot tell us whether you performed above or below other students. A picture advantage is a group-level research prediction, not a requirement for every individual.

Does this mean you have a particular learning style?

Not necessarily. Enjoying a learning format and remembering more from it are different things. Research has found little convincing evidence that matching teaching to a person's preferred visual or auditory learning style reliably improves learning. Your scores reflect short-term cued recall after a brief distraction, not your intelligence or general academic ability. With only six items per format, one extra remembered pair changes that format's score by about 17 percentage points. Word difficulty, order, concentration and picture naming can all influence the outcome.

What could you try the next time you study?

Whatever format you use, try closing your notes and retrieving information from memory, then checking your answers. Revisit the material across multiple days instead of relying only on rereading or listening. Choose a format that suits the content: diagrams for spatial relationships, words for definitions and audio for pronunciation, for example.

RESEARCH NOTES

What are we testing?

StudyLab asks whether immediate cued recall differs after information is presented as written pairs, picture-only pairs, or spoken pairs. Each session uses 18 preselected pairs, assigns six to each format, and randomizes the block order and list-to-format pairing. Each study round is followed by a 30-second distractor and six cued-recall questions.

Key limitation: preferences for visual, auditory, or written presentation are not established diagnostic “learning styles.” A single session cannot show that matching someone's preference to a format improves learning. Small item sets, short retention intervals, audio quality, and ambiguity in naming pictures and the use of written recall cues all limit interpretation.

Audio uses your browser's speech synthesis. It may sound different across devices; if unavailable, the app records a text fallback. The results are exploratory.
